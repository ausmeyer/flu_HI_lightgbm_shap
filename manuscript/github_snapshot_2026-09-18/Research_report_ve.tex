\begin{abstract}
\RaggedRight
Seasonal influenza A(H3N2) vaccine updates are guided primarily by antigenic measurements such as hemagglutination inhibition titers. Sequence data can help identify emerging hemagglutinin substitutions for serological follow-up, but published analyses disagree on which positions are most strongly associated with antigenic variation. Here, we trained gradient-boosted tree models to predict standardized titer reductions from paired virus and antiserum hemagglutinin sequences in the Neher/Bedford benchmark and a larger WHO Collaborating Centre dataset. We used feature-attribution scores to quantify the contributions of site mismatches, amino-acid states, and directed substitutions. With the Neher/Bedford models, we recovered the canonical cluster-transition positions. After filtering the Collaborating Centre data by passage history, we identified a related set of head-region positions from more contemporary observations. These included site 225, which a published structurally aware analysis had identified at high posterior inclusion probability. We also found overlap with published antigenic site lists and several rankings derived without HI measurements. We evaluated the models by retrospective cross-validation, keeping all observations of each named virus in the same fold. Sera and closely related viruses could occur in both training and test data, and we did not test performance on new clades or future seasons. We interpret the attributions as associations with predicted titers. Together with substitution-level tables and exploratory predictions of background-dependent titer changes, these results can help identify sequence changes for further serological study.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Seasonal influenza A(H3N2) vaccine strain selection is driven primarily by antigenic phenotype. Viruses are tested in hemagglutination inhibition (HI) assays against antisera raised against vaccine and reference strains, and these measurements guide vaccine updates~\cite{Russell2008Science, Bedford2015Nature, Smith2004Science}. Sequence data can help identify which viruses warrant serological follow-up. For example, a newly sequenced virus may carry a substitution at a position with established antigenic significance. The significance of a substitution at another position is harder to assess. A more complete understanding of which hemagglutinin (HA) positions, and which substitutions at those positions, are associated with HI titer change would help guide this testing.

Published sets of important sites disagree more than one might expect. Early crystallographic and antibody-mapping studies defined four antigenic regions on H3 hemagglutinin (sites A--D)~\cite{Wiley1981Nature}, and the canonical map is now often described as five regions (sites A--E)~\cite{Koel2013Science}. Investigators have used positive-selection analyses to predict H3N2 evolution~\cite{bush1999predicting} and cluster-transition analyses to identify a small number of important positions near the receptor-binding site~\cite{Koel2013Science}. With additive titer models built within a phylogenetic framework, Neher et al.\ implicated a broader set of positions~\cite{Neher2016PNAS}. Harvey et al.\ used a Bayesian structural-genomic analysis of WHO Collaborating Centre (WIC) HI data to rank positions by posterior inclusion probability~\cite{Harvey2023PLoSComputBiol}. Shah et al.\ trained a seasonal AdaBoost model on partially overlapping WIC data and identified 30 positions by aggregating feature importance across influenza seasons~\cite{shah2024seasonal}. These four reference sets (Koel, Neher/Bedford, Harvey, and Shah) share a core of sites, with less agreement elsewhere. The differences likely reflect the composition and time period of each dataset, the analytical methods, and the effects of passage on HI reactivity~\cite{Parker2016JGenVirol} and antibody recognition~\cite{Zost2017PNAS}.

Other investigators have used evolutionary variability as a proxy for antigenic importance. Sitewise dN/dS, the ratio of amino acid-changing to silent substitution rates, and Shannon entropy can identify rapidly evolving or unusually variable positions~\cite{fitch1991positive, NeiGojobori1986, suzuki2006natural, pan2011quantifying}. It is harder to interpret these quantities in terms of antigenic effect because variability can also reflect passage artifacts~\cite{McWhite2016VE_passage}, structural constraints that act independently of antigenic pressure~\cite{Meyer2015PP_geom, echave2016causes}, and artifacts in rate estimation at short divergence times~\cite{Meyer2015VE_time, Ho2005MBE}. Statistical and machine learning approaches allow investigators to relate sequence variation more directly to a phenotype. Sequence-based models have been used to predict antigenic phenotype~\cite{xia2021deep} and influenza fitness~\cite{Luksza2014Nature}, and to inform vaccine-strain prediction~\cite{Steinbruck2014JVirol}. Investigators have also used interpretable methods to prioritize residues from protein-sequence data~\cite{hie2021learning, thadani2023learning}. Here, we ask whether we can better understand the agreement and disagreement among existing H3N2 site lists by applying the same modeling framework and definitions of importance to more than one HI collection.

Most published rankings identify important positions, although different amino acid substitutions at the same position can have different antigenic effects. Koel et al.\ showed experimentally that substitutions at position 145 were context-independent in their reverse-genetics panel, whereas substitutions at position 155 were context-dependent~\cite{Koel2019JGenVirol}. By examining individual substitutions, we can describe these differences and distinguish changes that add or remove glycosylation potential. For surveillance, knowing that site 145 is important may therefore be less useful than understanding a specific change such as N145K in a given season.

Here we apply gradient-boosted tree models (LightGBM) with SHAP-based attribution~\cite{Lundberg2017, Lundberg2020NatMachIntell} to the Neher/Bedford H3N2 dataset~\cite{Neher2016PNAS} and the larger WIC H3N2 dataset~\cite{Harvey2023PLoSComputBiol}. At each HA position, we encode whether the virus and serum amino acids differ and which amino acid each carries. We also use a separate encoding for each directed substitution and calculate SHAP values to quantify each feature's contribution to the predicted titer. We compare the resulting rankings with the Koel, Neher/Bedford, Harvey, and Shah reference sets, independent rankings derived without HI measurements~\cite{Meyer2026Interface_sequenceML}, and standard evolutionary metrics. For Harvey, we use all 14 HA1 positions with posterior inclusion probability (PIP) at least 0.95 in the structurally aware model, including 225~\cite{Harvey2023PLoSComputBiol}. In the supplement, we computationally introduce Koel substitutions and site-160 glycosylation-motif changes to explore predicted titer changes across sequence backgrounds. We have not tested epistasis experimentally.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Interpretable models recover the canonical H3N2 cluster-transition sites from the Neher/Bedford dataset}

We first examined whether we could recover the canonical H3N2 cluster-transition sites from the Neher/Bedford data using two complementary LightGBM encodings. In the site-state model, we represented each HA position with a binary change indicator and separate one-hot encodings of the virus and serum amino acids. In the substitution model, we represented each observed directed amino-acid difference as a binary feature, such as a covariate for N145K. We included temporal distance in both models. To evaluate retrospective predictions for held-out viruses, we used five-fold cross-validation grouped by virus name, assigning all observations for a given virus to the same fold. We describe the scope of this evaluation in Methods.

After matching exact strain names to HA sequences, aligning the sequences, and applying quality control, we retained 8{,}599 HI measurements in the Neher/Bedford H3N2 dataset. The viruses and antisera spanned 1968 through 2011. We trained the models to predict residual standardized log\textsubscript{2} HI titers after removing per-serum and per-virus additive offsets (Table~\ref{tab:primary-model-summary}). The site-state model achieved a mean cross-validation Spearman correlation of 0.714 and RMSE of 1.971 log\textsubscript{2} titer units. We obtained similar performance with the substitution model, with a Spearman correlation of 0.704 and RMSE of 2.046. Both models performed better than the temporal-distance-only baseline (Spearman 0.587), indicating that HA sequence variation carries predictive information beyond calendar time.

We used SHAP values to quantify the contribution of each feature to the predicted titer for a given virus--serum pair~\cite{Lundberg2017, Lundberg2020NatMachIntell}. Sites with large mean absolute SHAP values were those whose sequence states most consistently predicted antigenic distance in the fitted model. Because HA substitutions can be correlated along clades, several sites can share this predictive signal. We therefore interpret the attributions as contributions to the model's predictions, with the independent causal effect of each site remaining unresolved.

The leading sites in the site-state model were head-region positions associated with H3N2 cluster transitions (Fig.~\ref{fig:h3n2-primary}), including 189, 145, 133, 135, 121, 158, 156, 193, and 144. Of the seven Koel cluster-transition positions (145, 155, 156, 158, 159, 189, 193)~\cite{Koel2013Science}, we recovered six among the site-state model's top 30 and all seven among the substitution model's top 30 (Table~S2). We also recovered 11 of the 15 Neher/Bedford reference positions compiled from substitutions discussed by Neher et al.~\cite{Neher2016PNAS} in the site-state top 30 and 14 in the substitution top 30. With both encodings, we recovered 12 of the 14 Harvey structurally aware PIP~$\geq$0.95 positions~\cite{Harvey2023PLoSComputBiol}. The site-state model also recovered 14 of 30 Shah positions (Table~S2). For comparison, two independently drawn sets of 30 from 550 mature-HA sites would overlap by 1.6 sites on average, with corresponding expectations of 0.4 for a 7-site list and 0.8 for a 15-site list. The leading positions had stable ranks across folds (Fig.~S3). We also evaluated the sequence models in a secondary holdout in which we withheld 10\% of virus names at once. The sequence models again outperformed the non-sequence baseline (Fig.~S1), although closely related viruses could still appear in the training and test data, as in the primary cross-validation. Fig.~S4 compares predicted and observed titers for the random-measurement and grouped-virus holdouts.

When we added patristic distance as a covariate, we found little change in performance (Spearman 0.715 and RMSE 1.973 with patristic distance, compared with 0.714 and 1.971 without; Table~S1) or in the leading sites (Fig.~S12, S13). Thus, we recovered the canonical cluster-transition site set from HI titers and HA sequences with the Neher/Bedford site-state model. We did so without structural data or an explicit phylogenetic correction.

\subsection{Passage filtering clarifies the biological signal in the WIC dataset}

We next applied the models to the larger WIC H3N2 dataset. These virus--serum HI observations span multiple decades, although most observations included in the models were collected after 2010. Of the 202{,}605 raw HI observations, we matched both virus and serum components to HA1 sequences for 104{,}972 (51.8\%), and retained 104{,}605 (51.6\%) after alignment quality control. Egg passage can alter measured HI reactivity~\cite{Parker2016JGenVirol} and antibody recognition~\cite{Zost2017PNAS}, making it harder to interpret the antigenic properties of the circulating virus.

We therefore filtered the WIC observations by passage history, excluding those whose normalized virus or reference passage class was exactly \texttt{EGG}, \texttt{MIXED}, or \texttt{UNKNOWN}. We retained 28{,}521 sequence-matched measurements after filtering, of which 28{,}448 passed alignment quality control (Table~S3). Under this rule, we retained observations with composite labels such as \texttt{CELL|EGG}. Excluding every label containing \texttt{EGG} would remove another 418 of 28{,}521 rows (1.5\%), leaving 28{,}103. We counted these additional exclusions but did not retrain under the stricter rule (Supplementary Methods S1.8). For the primary analysis, we used the exact-class filter. The resulting subset contains a higher proportion of recent, cell-associated observations, which makes interpretation clearer but reduces its representativeness.

In the passage-filtered WIC data, the site-state model achieved a mean cross-validation Spearman correlation of 0.782 and RMSE of 0.992 (Table~\ref{tab:primary-model-summary}; Table~S1). We obtained a higher Spearman correlation (0.809) and RMSE (1.234) with the unfiltered model. Because filtering changes the response distribution, we cannot compare these metrics directly across subsets. We can see the difference more clearly in the plots of predicted and observed titers. Predictions for the filtered data form a tighter distribution, without the two-cluster structure seen in the unfiltered data (Fig.~S6, S10). We use the filtered models as the primary analysis of the more contemporary observations.

The leading sites in the filtered site-state model were 144, 225, 159, 140, and 145. Sites 142, 62, 53, 45, 326, 197, 158, 193, 173, and 189 also appeared among the top 15 (Fig.~\ref{fig:wic-filtered-primary}). Site 144 belongs to antigenic region A and the Harvey PIP~$\geq$0.95 set~\cite{Harvey2023PLoSComputBiol}. Site 225 ranked second in our analysis. Harvey et al.\ also included this site among their 14 structurally aware PIP~$\geq$0.95 positions and classified it as part of the receptor-binding site. The leading sites had stable ranks across folds (Fig.~S7), and we compare standardized with context-corrected titers in Fig.~S8. In a secondary grouped-virus holdout, we also found that the sequence models outperformed the context-only baseline (Fig.~S5). We compare predicted and observed titers for the random-measurement and grouped-virus holdouts in Fig.~S9.

We found noisier rankings in the unfiltered WIC data, with passage-associated positions appearing more prominently alongside canonical antigenic sites (Fig.~S10, S11). When we added patristic distance to the filtered site-state model, performance improved only slightly (Spearman 0.787 versus 0.782, RMSE 0.982 versus 0.992; Table~S1). Our ability to interpret the rankings therefore depended more on which observations we retained than on whether we included a phylogenetic correction.

\subsection{The models explain predictions at the level of specific amino-acid states and substitutions}

We next examined the feature-level SHAP values to distinguish the contributions of amino-acid states and directed substitutions at each position. In Figs.~\ref{fig:h3n2-primary} and \ref{fig:wic-filtered-primary}, we combined these components into site-level scores. Tables~S4 and S5 provide the complete results for each feature value.

In the Neher/Bedford analysis, a mismatch at site 189 was the most influential site-state feature. Across 2{,}823 observations, this feature contributed a mean SHAP of 0.899 log\textsubscript{2} titer units toward greater antigenic distance. Among the substitutions observed at that site, S189R had the largest positive contribution (mean SHAP 1.031; $n=254$). At site 158, N158K had the largest positive substitution-level contribution in that model (mean SHAP 1.561; $n=188$). We also examined the signs of the contributions. For example, K193S was associated with reduced predicted titer loss (mean SHAP $-1.188$; $n=92$). K383R, at a site in HA2, had the largest absolute substitution-level SHAP in the same table (mean SHAP $-1.655$; $n=46$). These 46 observations are assay measurements and can include repeated observations of a virus. We retained sparse substitutions and their observation counts in the complete tables.

In the filtered WIC substitution model, K189N had the largest positive contribution (mean SHAP 1.724; $n=67$ in the 12{,}500-row SHAP subsample). We also observed contributions with opposite signs for H197Q ($-0.833$; $n=187$) and N225D ($+0.831$; $n=1{,}099$). At site 145, N145S had the largest contribution ($+0.323$; $n=1{,}642$). Fig.~S16 and Fig.~S17 show the top 20 signed contributions for individual feature values in both primary models. These values describe how each feature contributes to a prediction from the fitted trees, and we have not measured the corresponding experimental effect sizes.

\subsection{We find agreement among model rankings and published H3N2 site lists}

We compared the top 30 sites from each primary site-state model with the four benchmark sets and external rankings derived without HI measurements (Fig.~\ref{fig:cross-study-concordance}). The reference sets share a core of sites, with more limited pairwise overlap elsewhere. For example, 13 of the 14 Harvey PIP~$\geq$0.95 sites appear in the Shah set, giving a union of 31. We report the benchmark overlap counts in Table~S2. In Table~S6, we show which published lists contain each site in the union of the two models' top 15 sites.

Among the Neher/Bedford site-state model's top 30 sites, we recovered 6 of 7 Koel positions, 11 of 15 Neher/\allowbreak Bedford positions, 12 of 14 Harvey PIP~$\geq$0.95 positions, and 14 of 30 Shah positions. Among the filtered WIC site-state model's top 30, we recovered 5 of 7 Koel positions, 10 of 15 Neher/Bedford positions, 11 of 14 Harvey positions, and 17 of 30 Shah positions (Table~S2). We also report a sensitivity comparison with a restricted 15-site Harvey variant that omits 225 in the machine-readable data accompanying Table~S2.

We also found substantial overlap between the filtered WIC top 30 and rankings derived without HI measurements. We compared our ranking with rankings from models that used LightGBM+SHAP to predict passage history and collection date~\cite{Meyer2026Interface_sequenceML}, and found 16 and 18 of 30 shared positions, respectively. We found 20 of 30 shared positions when we compared it with the most variable positions in unpassaged H3N2 sequences, measured by Shannon entropy (Fig.~\ref{fig:cross-study-concordance}B). The overlap was weaker for sitewise dN/dS. We found 10 of 30 shared positions with the unpassaged dN/dS ranking and 6 of 30 with the McWhite et al.\ unpassaged ranking~\cite{McWhite2016VE_passage}.

We reached similar conclusions in two sensitivity analyses. When we included all unfiltered WIC observations, we obtained a noisier ranking (Fig.~S10, S11). Adding patristic distance had little effect on RMSE, and we found substantial overlap among the leading sites (Table~S1; Fig.~S12--S15). In Supplementary Results Section D, we report exploratory predictions of background-dependent titer changes at Koel sites 145 and 155 and at the HA1 158--160 glycosylation motif.

\section{Discussion}

We found that, with gradient-boosted tree models and SHAP attribution, we could recover a large fraction of the positions identified in the Koel, Neher/Bedford, Harvey, and Shah H3N2 analyses. We also quantified the contributions of amino-acid states and directed substitutions at those positions. In the Neher/Bedford analysis, we recovered nearly all canonical cluster-transition sites~\cite{Koel2013Science, Neher2016PNAS}. With the filtered WIC model, we recovered 11 of Harvey's 14 structurally aware PIP~$\geq$0.95 sites, including 225, and 17 of Shah's 30 sites~\cite{Harvey2023PLoSComputBiol, shah2024seasonal}. Harvey and Shah share 13 of those 14 Harvey sites. By applying the same framework to two HI collections, we were able to compare site, state, and substitution attributions using a common definition of each reference set.

For each site, we quantify the contributions of the amino-acid mismatch between the test virus and the serum-raised virus, the amino acid each virus carries, and each directed substitution. We use SHAP to assign a signed contribution to each component~\cite{Lundberg2017, Lundberg2020NatMachIntell}. In additive titer models, including the Neher/Bedford framework, genetic contributions to antigenic change are represented as a sum of independent branch or substitution effects~\cite{Neher2016PNAS}. This approach works well when effects are approximately additive. With Bayesian structural-genomic models, investigators can also incorporate phylogenetic and structural information and account for uncertainty more formally, as Harvey et al.\ did~\cite{Harvey2023PLoSComputBiol}. Tree models can represent interactions through their splits, and we can then use SHAP to decompose a prediction into contributions from individual features. We interpret these contributions in relation to the fitted predictor.

We can interpret the attributions at site 225 alongside findings from earlier work. This site ranked second in the filtered WIC site-state model and first in the corresponding substitution model, where N225D had a mean SHAP of 0.831 across 1{,}099 observations in the SHAP subsample. Harvey et al.\ included 225 among the 14 structurally aware PIP~$\geq$0.95 positions and assigned it to the receptor-binding site~\cite{Harvey2023PLoSComputBiol}. Chambers et al.\ listed N225D among HA differences separating 2014--2015 3C.2a and 3C.3a viruses from A/Texas/50/2012. In their reverse-genetics panel, they identified antigenic-site-B mutations as the principal drivers of that mismatch~\cite{Chambers2015CellReports}. Our high SHAP rank therefore agrees with Harvey's PIP ranking and with the presence of N225D in those drift backgrounds. We interpret this agreement as observational and predictive support. Whether 225 itself constitutes a classical epitope remains unresolved by these comparisons.

The agreement we found with rankings derived without HI measurements~\cite{Meyer2026Interface_sequenceML} is consistent with the prioritized sites being evolutionarily active. Sites could also rank highly in several analyses because of shared confounding and correlated substitution patterns. We would expect weaker overlap with dN/dS if it identifies diversifying selection without distinguishing antigenic consequence~\cite{fitch1991positive, suzuki2006natural, echave2016causes, Meyer2015PP_geom, Meyer2013MBE}. In our analysis, we calculate SHAP importance for a measured HI outcome. This allows us to rank substitutions by their association with predicted titers, which more closely matches the question posed in surveillance.

We also found that the rankings were clearer after we filtered the WIC data by exact passage class. This is consistent with work showing that egg adaptation confounds residue-level analyses~\cite{Bush2000PNAS_passage, McWhite2016VE_passage, Parker2016JGenVirol, Zost2017PNAS, kistler2025MBE}. We retained composite egg labels, which account for 1.5\% of the rows kept by the primary filter, and did not retrain the models with these rows excluded. When we added patristic distance, we obtained at most a marginal improvement. A flexible nonlinear model can largely account for phylogenetic structure already present in the sequence features.

Both HI collections reflect surveillance sampling and assay preferences. In the filtered WIC analysis, we retained 28{,}448 of the 104{,}605 sequence-matched observations that passed alignment quality control. We used grouped cross-validation to evaluate retrospective predictions for held-out virus names. We did not test performance in future seasons. Because highly ranked sites can reflect correlated clade structure, sampling, or assay context, we interpret agreement with the four reference sets as concordance across analyses. The Koel 2013 cluster-transition sites have an experimental basis. The Koel 2019 experiments at 145/155 also provide context for the exploratory predictions in the supplement, although we did not reproduce those experiments as a test of the direction of their effects. We did not model glycosylation occupancy~\cite{Das2011PNAS_glycan, Kosik2018PLoSPathog, Altman2019mBio, Zost2017PNAS}. In the complete tables, we retained sparse substitutions such as K383R together with their observation counts. For the exploratory Koel counterfactuals in the supplement, we report predictions from both full-data fits and cross-validation fits. The main performance table reports cross-validation results.

For surveillance, we would give greatest priority for serological follow-up to positions that rank highly across both datasets, both encodings, and multiple reference comparisons. Differences between the datasets may reflect variation over time or differences specific to each collection. Together with deep mutational scanning~\cite{Lee2018PNAS, Thyagarajan2014eLife} and antigenic cartography~\cite{Smith2004Science}, the rankings and tables of individual feature values can help direct experiments toward substitutions most likely to be associated with HI titer change~\cite{Neher2016PNAS, Bedford2015Nature}.

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Data sources and sequence processing}

We obtained the Neher/Bedford H3N2 HI dataset and matched HA sequences from the Neher/Bedford antigenic and phylogenetic analysis framework~\cite{Neher2016PNAS}. We assembled the WIC H3N2 HI dataset and passage annotations from the WHO Collaborating Centre surveillance data described in Harvey et al.~\cite{Harvey2023PLoSComputBiol}.

For the Neher/Bedford dataset, we retrieved HA sequences from GISAID~\cite{Elbe2017GlobChall, Shu2017EuroSurveillance} using isolate identifiers matched to the HI records. We used NCBI GenBank as a fallback for isolates unresolved through GISAID. We normalized strain names to uppercase alphanumeric characters and required an exact match between normalized names. After alignment quality control, we retained 8{,}599 HI observations and 402 HA sequence records corresponding to virus names. These records represented 338 distinct aligned amino-acid strings.

The 202{,}605 raw WIC HI observations included 16{,}223 unique virus and reference serum strains. We searched for their sequences in several stages. We first searched the curated HA1 alignment distributed with Harvey et al.~\cite{Harvey2023PLoSComputBiol}, where we found 1{,}738 matched sequences. We then searched GISAID manually by strain name~\cite{Elbe2017GlobChall, Shu2017EuroSurveillance} and obtained 2{,}501 additional strain-level matches. We used NCBI GenBank/IRD and BV-BRC as fallback sources. The final modeling inputs contained 2{,}501 GISAID sequences and 1{,}738 Harvey repository sequences, representing 4{,}239 resolved strains. We therefore had sequences for both components of 104{,}972 HI observations, of which 104{,}605 passed alignment quality control. When a strain name matched multiple GISAID isolates, we selected a canonical isolate by preferring HA1-complete sequences ($\geq 328$ residues), then lower-passage material (unpassaged $>$ cell $>$ egg), lower sequence ambiguity, and finally the most common variant among HA1-complete candidates.

For the Neher/Bedford dataset, we aligned HA protein sequences to A/\allowbreak HongKong/\allowbreak 1/\allowbreak 1968 using MAFFT~\cite{Katoh2013MolBiolEvol} in automatic mode, removed the N-terminal 16-residue signal peptide, and retained the 550-residue mature HA sequence. For WIC, we extracted HA1 sequences, aligned them with MAFFT, and mapped them to mature HA positions. We excluded sequences with more than 5\% gaps or more than 2\% ambiguous residues. Supplementary Methods S1.1 and S1.2 provide full details.

\subsection{Titer preprocessing and avidity/potency correction}

We first standardized each titer against the homologous titer of the corresponding serum, so that larger values indicate greater loss of cross-reactivity against heterologous viruses. Before log\textsubscript{2} transformation, we converted censored values reported as $<$X or $>$X to X/2 and 2X, respectively.

For Neher/Bedford, we applied an additive correction with serum identity and virus strain as categorical factors before interpreting sequence features. For WIC, we used passage history to define a filtered subset and to account for laboratory-specific and assay-specific titer offsets. We excluded observations when either the virus or reference serum had a normalized passage class exactly equal to \texttt{EGG}, \texttt{MIXED}, or \texttt{UNKNOWN}. We retained 28{,}521 sequence-matched observations, including 28{,}448 with aligned HA1 sequences. We then fitted an additive avidity/potency model jointly by ordinary least squares, using reference serum identity, assay-date label, virus passage class, reference serum passage class, antiserum class, and red blood cell type as six categorical factors. We re-estimated the additive layer within each training split. Supplementary Methods S1.3 provide full details.

\subsection{Feature encodings and model fitting}

We applied two complementary sequence encodings to each dataset, covering the 550-residue mature HA sequence for Neher/Bedford and the 328-residue HA1 alignment for WIC. For the site-state encoding, we represented each position with a change indicator and separate one-hot encodings of the virus and serum amino-acid states. For the substitution encoding, we represented each observed directed amino-acid change from serum to virus as a binary indicator. We included temporal distance, defined as the absolute calendar-year difference, in the Neher/Bedford models. In the WIC models, we included both temporal distance and assay-context covariates. We added patristic distance in sensitivity analyses. Supplementary Methods S1.4 describe the encodings in full.

We trained gradient-boosted tree models using LightGBM~\cite{Ke2017LightGBM} with up to 1{,}000 boosting iterations, a learning rate of 0.1, 31 leaves, a minimum of 20 child samples, and no L1 or L2 regularization. Within each training split, we selected the number of boosting rounds by withholding 20\% of the training virus names in an inner group-based split for early stopping. We then evaluated performance by five-fold cross-validation grouped by virus name. We kept all observations of a named virus in one fold, although sera could appear in several folds and closely related viruses could appear in both training and test data. We used this procedure to evaluate retrospective predictions for held-out virus names. We did not evaluate performance on new clades or future seasons. In the secondary holdout analyses, we withheld either 10\% of measurements at random or 10\% of virus names at once. We compare RMSE across model families in Figs.~S1 and S5, and site-state predictions with observed titers in Figs.~S4 and S9. Supplementary Methods S1.5 describe the validation procedures.

\subsection{Patristic distance computation}

We estimated maximum-likelihood phylogenies from the aligned mature-HA sequences for Neher/Bedford and the aligned HA1 sequences for WIC using FastTree~\cite{Price2010PLoSOne} under the WAG amino-acid substitution model with gamma-distributed rate variation (\texttt{-wag -gamma}). We extracted pairwise patristic distances with Biopython~\cite{cock2009biopython} and included them in sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Methods S1.8).

\subsection{SHAP attribution, value-level tables, and site ranking}

We computed SHAP values~\cite{Lundberg2017} for all site-state and substitution features using TreeExplainer~\cite{Lundberg2020NatMachIntell}. For Neher/Bedford, we aggregated values over all held-out predictions in each cross-validation fold. For the larger WIC analyses, we sampled up to 2{,}500 held-out rows per fold, giving a maximum of 12{,}500 contributing rows in Table~S5. We calculated site importance by summing mean absolute SHAP values across the change, virus-state, and serum-state feature groups at each site. The value-level tables (Tables~S4 and S5) report the mean signed contribution of each covariate value and its number of contributing observations. We assessed rank stability across folds separately (Figs.~S3, S7). Supplementary Methods S1.6 provide full details.

\subsection{Comparisons with published site lists}

We compared the model rankings with four reference sets. The Koel set contains positions 145, 155, 156, 158, 159, 189, and 193~\cite{Koel2013Science}. We compiled the 15 Neher/Bedford positions from substitutions discussed by Neher et al.~\cite{Neher2016PNAS}. For Harvey, we used all 14 HA1 positions with PIP at least 0.95 in the structurally aware model, namely 131, 135, 138, 144, 145, 157, 158, 159, 173, 189, 193, 194, 223, and 225~\cite{Harvey2023PLoSComputBiol}. We used a restricted 15-site Harvey variant that omits 225 only for a sensitivity comparison. The Shah set contains 30 positions identified by aggregating the top 20 feature-importance sites across 14 influenza seasons~\cite{shah2024seasonal}. We also used two LightGBM+SHAP rankings derived from passage-history and collection-date prediction without HI measurements~\cite{Meyer2026Interface_sequenceML}, together with unpassaged sequence entropy and dN/dS rankings~\cite{McWhite2016VE_passage, Meyer2015PP_geom}. For each comparison, we counted the positions shared between the model's top 30 and the reference set. Supplementary Methods S1.7 give the hypergeometric expectations. We report overlap with the benchmark sets in Table~S2 and overlap with the external rankings in Fig.~\ref{fig:cross-study-concordance}B.
