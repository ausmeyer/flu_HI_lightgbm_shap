\section*{Supplementary Methods}
%%% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection*{S1.1\quad Data sources and inclusion criteria}

\textbf{Neher/Bedford H3N2 dataset.} We used the curated Neher/Bedford H3N2 hemagglutination inhibition dataset, which spans viruses and antisera from 1968 through 2011~\cite{Neher2016PNAS}. After matching virus strains to HA protein sequences and applying alignment quality control, we retained 8,599 HI observations and 402 named-virus HA sequence records. Multiple records can share the same amino-acid sequence.

\textbf{WIC H3N2 dataset.} We assembled the WIC H3N2 dataset from WHO Collaborating Centre surveillance HI measurements~\cite{Harvey2023PLoSComputBiol}. Of the 202,605 raw HI observations, 104,972 had matched HA1 sequences for both the virus and serum components. After alignment quality control, we retained 104,605 observations for modeling (S1.2).

\textbf{WIC filtered subset.} We excluded observations in which either the virus or reference serum had a normalized passage class exactly equal to \texttt{EGG}, \texttt{MIXED}, or \texttt{UNKNOWN}. Composite labels such as \texttt{CELL|EGG} and \texttt{CELL|UNKNOWN} remained in the filtered subset because they differ from the exact excluded classes. We retained 28,521 sequence-matched observations under this filter. Of these, 28,448 had aligned HA1 sequences available for feature construction. Table~S3 summarizes the retained and excluded observations. We describe the stricter composite-egg filter in S1.8.

\subsection*{S1.2\quad Sequence acquisition, alignment, and numbering}

\textbf{Neher/Bedford sequence retrieval.} We retrieved HA protein sequences from GISAID~\cite{Elbe2017GlobChall, Shu2017EuroSurveillance} using isolate identifiers matched to the HI table. For strains unresolved in GISAID, we queried NCBI GenBank through the Entrez API. We normalized strain names by converting them to uppercase and removing non-alphanumeric characters, then required an exact match between the normalized HI and sequence names. This rule excluded near-name matches and removed 1,460 of the 10,059 input HI rows. All matched rows in the final analysis met the exact-match rule.

\textbf{WIC sequence retrieval.} The WIC HI dataset references 16,223 unique virus and reference serum strains. We searched three sequence sources in the following order.

\textit{(1) Harvey et al.\ curated HA1 alignment.} We first searched the curated HA1 alignment distributed with Harvey et al.\ (2023)~\cite{Harvey2023PLoSComputBiol} at \url{https://github.com/will-harvey/Flu_g2p_mapping}. This alignment provided sequences for 1,738 strains.

\textit{(2) GISAID.} We manually searched GISAID EpiFlu for strains unresolved in the curated alignment. When several isolates matched a strain name, we preferred HA1-complete sequences ($\geq$328 residues), then lower-passage material, then lower sequence ambiguity, and finally the most common variant among HA1-complete candidates. This search resolved an additional 2,501 strains. Four records retained an IRD accession-database annotation; we counted them among the 2,501 GISAID records because GISAID supplied their selected sequences.

\textit{(3) Public databases.} We queried NCBI GenBank and BV-BRC for the remaining strains. Matching produced 20 accession-linked manifest rows. The final FASTA used for modeling contained sequences from the Harvey repository and GISAID only.

In total, we resolved HA1 sequences for 4,239 of the 16,223 unique strains (26\%). These sequences covered both components of 104,972 of the 202,605 raw HI observations (51.8\%). After alignment quality control, 104,605 observations remained (51.6\% of the raw observations).

\textbf{Alignment, quality control, and numbering.} We aligned the Neher/Bedford HA protein sequences to A/HongKong/1/1968 using MAFFT~\cite{Katoh2013MolBiolEvol} in automatic mode (\texttt{--auto}), removed the N-terminal 16-residue signal peptide, and retained the 550-residue mature HA sequence. For WIC, we extracted HA1 sequences using length-based cutoffs, aligned them with MAFFT, and mapped them to mature HA numbering. We excluded sequences with more than 5\% gap positions or more than 2\% ambiguous residues (X, B, Z, or J). Mature-protein positions equal gene-site positions minus 16; for example, gene site 176 corresponds to mature site 160.

\subsection*{S1.3\quad Titer preprocessing and avidity/potency correction}

\textbf{Censored titer handling.} We converted titers reported as $<$X to X/2 and titers reported as $>$X to 2X, then log\textsubscript{2}-transformed the values.

\textbf{Homologous titer normalization.} We standardized each observation by subtracting its log\textsubscript{2} titer from the homologous log\textsubscript{2} titer. When a homologous titer was unavailable, we used the maximum observed titer across all viruses tested against that serum. This fallback was used for 999 of 8,599 Neher/Bedford observations (11.6\%) and 1,540 of 28,521 sequence-matched filtered WIC observations (5.4\%). The WIC denominator precedes alignment quality control.

\textbf{Additive avidity/potency correction for WIC.} We specified an additive least-squares model with six categorical factors. These were reference serum identifier, assay date label, virus passage class, reference serum passage class, antiserum class, and red blood cell type. We re-estimated the additive effects within each training split (Fig.~S8).

\subsection*{S1.4\quad Model specification and feature encodings}

\textbf{Site-state encoding.} We represented each modeled position with a binary change indicator, a one-hot virus amino-acid state, and a one-hot serum amino-acid state. The Neher/Bedford features span the 550-residue mature HA sequence, and the WIC features span the 328-residue HA1 alignment.

\textbf{Substitution encoding.} We represented each observed directed amino acid change from serum to virus at each modeled position with a binary indicator. Sites with no observed change had no separate indicator.

\textbf{Distance covariates.} We included temporal distance in the Neher/Bedford models. WIC models included temporal distance and 9 assay-context covariates. These were virus passage class, reference passage class, virus passage known/unknown, reference passage known/unknown, antiserum class, red blood cell type, red blood cell species, oseltamivir presence, and oseltamivir concentration inferred from the red blood cell annotation.

\textbf{Model fitting.} We trained gradient-boosted tree models with LightGBM~\cite{Ke2017LightGBM}, allowing up to 1,000 boosting iterations. We used a learning rate of 0.1, 31 leaves, an unconstrained maximum tree depth, a minimum of 20 child samples, and no L1 or L2 regularization. Within each training fold, we reserved 20\% of training virus names in an inner grouped split for early stopping, with a patience of 50 rounds. We used only the inner split for early stopping and reserved the outer held-out fold for evaluation.

\subsection*{S1.5\quad Validation and held-out evaluation}

\textbf{Grouped cross-validation.} We assigned all HI observations for a given virus name to the same cross-validation fold, regardless of the serum used in the assay. Thus, named viruses were separated between training and test data, while sera and closely related viruses could occur in both. We evaluated retrospective predictions for held-out virus names. We did not evaluate generalization to new clades or future seasons. We used five-fold grouped cross-validation for all primary analyses and summarized performance as the mean Spearman correlation and RMSE between predicted and observed standardized titers across held-out folds.

\textbf{Secondary holdout diagnostics.} We also evaluated single splits that withheld either a random 10\% of HI observations or all observations involving a grouped 10\% of virus names. Related viruses could occur on both sides of the grouped-virus split, as in the primary cross-validation. Figures~S1, S5, S13, and S15 compare model families under these two schemes. Figures~S4 and S9 show the corresponding site-state predictions, and Table~S1 reports the grouped-cross-validation metrics.

\subsection*{S1.6\quad SHAP attribution, value-level tables, and site-ranking stability}

We computed SHAP values with TreeExplainer~\cite{Lundberg2020NatMachIntell} for the fitted LightGBM models. For Neher/Bedford, we aggregated SHAP values over all held-out predictions across folds. For WIC, we sampled up to 2,500 held-out rows per fold, giving a maximum of 12,500 rows in Table~S5. We calculated site importance by summing the mean absolute SHAP values for the site-change, virus-state, and serum-state groups. Tables~S4 and S5 give the mean signed SHAP value and number of contributing observations for each value-level feature, and Figs.~S3 and S7 show fold-to-fold stability. The complete Neher/Bedford substitution table includes the HA2 substitution K383R at site 383 ($n=46$ assay rows); we retained all entries without applying a post hoc count cutoff.

\subsection*{S1.7\quad Cross-study comparison design}

We compared the primary model rankings with the following reference sets and rankings in main-text Fig.~3.

\textbf{Koel reference set.} We used the seven positions (145, 155, 156, 158, 159, 189, 193) identified by Koel et al.\ (2013)~\cite{Koel2013Science}.

\textbf{Neher/Bedford reference set.} We compiled fifteen positions from the substitutions associated with cluster transitions in Neher et al.\ (2016)~\cite{Neher2016PNAS}. The 15-position list is our compilation of those published substitutions.

\textbf{Harvey reference set (primary).} We included all 14 HA1 positions with posterior inclusion probability at least 0.95 in the structurally aware model of Harvey et al.\ (2023)~\cite{Harvey2023PLoSComputBiol}. These positions are 131, 135, 138, 144, 145, 157, 158, 159, 173, 189, 193, 194, 223, and 225. We used this published selection rule for all main-text Harvey comparisons.

\textbf{Harvey restricted 15-site subset (sensitivity only).} This alternative subset combines naive-model PIP~$\geq$0.95 sites with top-25 posterior-inclusion sites in canonical antigenic regions. It omits 225 and covers only part of Harvey's identification. We report its overlaps separately in the Table~S2 TSV column \texttt{harvey\_restricted15\_top30\_overlap}; the primary comparison uses the complete structurally aware PIP~$\geq$0.95 set above.

\textbf{Shah reference set.} We used the thirty positions obtained by aggregating the top 20 feature-importance sites across 14 influenza seasons in Shah et al.\ (2024)~\cite{shah2024seasonal}. The Shah model uses WIC data from a similar period, so this comparison measures concordance between related WIC-derived analyses.

\textbf{LightGBM passage-SHAP and date-SHAP rankings.} We used the top-30 site rankings from LightGBM passage-history classifiers and collection-date regressors trained without HI measurements~\cite{Meyer2026Interface_sequenceML}.

\textbf{Unpassaged entropy and dN/dS.} We used site-wise Shannon entropy and dN/dS rankings from unpassaged H3N2 HA sequences, including the unpassaged-tip ranking of McWhite et al.\ (2016)~\cite{McWhite2016VE_passage}.

We used the top 30 sites from each primary model as a fixed comparison window. Under a hypergeometric null, the expected overlap is 2.7 sites for two sets of 30 drawn from the 328 WIC HA1 sites, 0.6 for a 7-site list, 1.4 for a 15-site list, and 1.3 for a 14-site list. The corresponding Neher/Bedford expectations over 550 mature-HA sites are 1.6, 0.4, and 0.8. Main-text Fig.~3B reports overlaps with rankings obtained without HI measurements; Table~S2 reports the benchmark-list comparisons. Table~S6 shows literature-list membership for the union of the top 15 sites from each primary model. Its notes refer to the experimental reports of Koel et al., Zost et al., and Chambers et al.~\cite{Koel2019JGenVirol, Zost2017PNAS, Chambers2015CellReports}. These membership comparisons describe concordance across studies. Experimental validation of individual antigenic effects requires separate evidence.

\subsection*{S1.8\quad Sensitivity analyses, patristic distance,\\ and passage-filter variants}

\textbf{Unfiltered WIC.} We analyzed the unfiltered WIC observations as a sensitivity analysis that retains passage heterogeneity (Figs.~S10--S11).

\textbf{Patristic-distance models.} We extracted pairwise patristic distances from FastTree WAG+gamma trees with Biopython~\cite{Price2010PLoSOne, cock2009biopython} and included them as a continuous covariate. Because these distances derive from the HA sequences already used as features, we treated the models as sensitivity analyses (Figs.~S12--S15). RMSE improved by at most about 1\% (Table~S1).

\textbf{Exact-class versus composite-egg filters.} The primary filter removes the exact classes \texttt{EGG}, \texttt{MIXED}, and \texttt{UNKNOWN}. Among the 28,521 sequence-matched filtered rows, 418 (1.47\%) retain \texttt{EGG} in a composite label on the virus or reference side. These labels are \texttt{CELL|EGG} and \texttt{EGG|CELL}. Removing these rows would leave 28,103 observations. Table~S3 includes the retained composite classes. We did not retrain under the stricter rule, so its effect on top-site rankings remains unknown. The retained 1.5\% row fraction also means that the primary filtered analysis includes some records with egg exposure.

\subsection*{S1.9\quad Counterfactual analyses at Koel sites 145 and 155}

Koel et al.\ (2019)~\cite{Koel2019JGenVirol} found that substitutions at HA1 position 145 were fully independent of amino-acid context in their reverse-genetics panel, whereas substitutions at position 155 varied with context. We used those substitutions to explore background dependence in the predictions of our fitted HI models.

We evaluated the exact single-residue substitutions assayed by Koel et al.\ at positions 145 and 155 in the seven Koel backbone viruses present in the Neher/Bedford HA alignment. The 17 wildtype-to-mutant contrasts per model scope represent 7 unique substitutions (H155T, H155Y, K145N, N145K, S145K, T155Y, Y155T) across 7 backgrounds. We changed only the target site for each contrast and encoded both sequences as site-state feature vectors against each of 152 serum sequence contexts. This gave 2,584 wildtype-to-mutant pairs. Three additional mutant-to-mutant contrasts contributed 456 pairs, giving 20 contrasts and 3,040 baseline--comparison pairs per model scope. We defined the predicted effect as the difference in predicted residual titer.

We evaluated full-data LightGBM fits, five-fold grouped cross-validation fits, and an additive ridge-regression comparator (alpha = 1.0) using the same features. The ridge model assigns a single weight to each site-state feature, giving each substitution one coefficient. Its background-level medians combine this background-invariant substitution effect with the other encoded states. The LightGBM contrasts describe exploratory predicted titer changes across backgrounds (Fig.~S18). We restricted the interpretation of signed differences from Koel HI titers to the behavior of the fitted models.

In the full-data LightGBM fit, the wildtype-to-mutant prediction for N145K in the SI87 background (A/Netherlands/620/1989) ranged from $-2.032$ to $+1.538$ log\textsubscript{2} units across serum contexts, with a median of $-0.091$. The corresponding ridge median was $-0.187$ in every background where N145K was encoded. Background medians varied more at site 145 than at site 155 in the full-data LightGBM fit (standard deviation 0.061 versus 0.020). Cross-validation medians for N145K followed a similar sign pattern (SI87 $-0.070$; VI75 $+0.019$; TX77 $-0.009$), providing a check on the stability of the model predictions.

\subsection*{S1.10\quad Counterfactual analysis at the HA1 158--160 glycosylation motif}

Zost et al.\ (2017)~\cite{Zost2017PNAS} showed that K160T introduces an N-linked glycosylation motif at HA1 positions 158--160 in contemporary H3N2 and that this motif alters antibody binding. Das et al.\ (2011), Kosik et al.\ (2018), and Altman et al.\ (2019)~\cite{Das2011PNAS_glycan, Kosik2018PLoSPathog, Altman2019mBio} provide broader evidence on glycosylation. Using the filtered WIC site-state model, we identified observed assay rows with K or T at site 160 and unambiguous residues at 158--160 (25,701 rows; 3,198 unique virus backgrounds). We changed only site 160 (K160T or T160K) and grouped the substitutions according to whether they created or removed the N158-X159-S/T160 motif. Motif-changing K160T had a larger predicted effect than motif-preserving controls, with median background-median effects of 0.056 versus 0.023 across 2,184 versus 347 backgrounds. The corresponding values for T160K were 0.091 versus 0.078 across 638 versus 29 backgrounds (Fig.~S19). These contrasts describe model predictions of titer change. Glycosylation occupancy and antigenic effects require experimental measurement.

\section*{Supplementary Results}
%%% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection*{Section A: Primary Neher/Bedford H3N2 diagnostics}

The Neher/Bedford LightGBM models predicted standardized HI titers under both random-observation and grouped named-virus holdout (Figs.~S1 and S4). Figure~S2 shows the pooled predictions from five-fold grouped cross-validation. The leading site rankings were stable across cross-validation folds (Fig.~S3). Figure~S16 shows the top 20 value-level features by absolute mean signed SHAP contribution.

\subsection*{Section B: Primary filtered WIC H3N2 diagnostics}

The filtered WIC models also predicted standardized HI titers under both holdout schemes (Figs.~S5 and S9). Figure~S6 shows the pooled predictions from five-fold grouped cross-validation. Their leading site rankings were largely stable across folds (Fig.~S7). Figure~S8 shows the additive context correction, and Fig.~S17 gives the corresponding value-level features.

\subsection*{Section C: Sensitivity analyses}

We observed a two-cluster titer distribution in the unfiltered WIC analysis. Positions associated with passage were also more prominent among the ranked sites (Figs.~S10 and S11). Adding patristic distance produced at most a marginal improvement in RMSE (Table~S1; Figs.~S12--S15), with changes in rank order and benchmark overlap (Table~S2).

\subsection*{Section D: Exploratory predicted titer changes}

We explored predicted titer changes after introducing Koel 145/155 substitutions and site-160 motif-changing substitutions into the fitted models (Figs.~S18 and S19). The predictions varied across sequence backgrounds. These exploratory comparisons describe the behavior of the fitted trees; experimental epistasis and agreement with Koel HI titers remain separate questions (S1.9, S1.10).
